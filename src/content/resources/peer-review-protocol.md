Use this for a literary paragraph, a full essay, or a section of an AP Seminar report. The writer leaves with a revision plan, not a cloud of reactions.

## Norms

- Talk about the writing, not the person.
- Be specific. “Confusing” needs a sentence attached to it.
- Do not rewrite the draft in the author’s voice.
- The writer owns the decision about what to change.

## The round (about 15 minutes)

**1. Silent read, 4 minutes.** The reviewer reads the whole piece once without a pen, then again with one. No comments yet.

**2. Say-back, 2 minutes.** The reviewer states the line of reasoning in two or three sentences: “You seem to be arguing that…, because…, and then….” The writer may say only “yes,” “not quite,” or correct a factual miss. No defending.

**3. Written notes, 4 minutes.** The reviewer answers only these prompts:

- One place the evidence is actually doing work:
- One place commentary is missing, thin, or talking about something else:
- One question I still have:

**4. Conversation, 3 minutes.** The reviewer reads the notes. The writer asks one clarifying question.

**5. Revision plan, 2 minutes.** The writer writes a single next move: the paragraph they will rebuild, the claim they will narrow, or the source they will reread. The plan is a sentence, not a promise to “add more detail.”

## If you are the writer

If the say-back is not the argument you thought you wrote, believe the say-back long enough to check. Readers report the argument that made it to the page.

## If you are the reviewer

You are not the teacher and you are not a copy editor. Grammar comments wait until the line of reasoning is visible. A clean sentence that argues the wrong thing is not the priority.
