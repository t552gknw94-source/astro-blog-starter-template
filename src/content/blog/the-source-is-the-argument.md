A paragraph can sound finished and still be empty. That was true before generative tools. It is harder to spot now, because the sentences arrive already smooth. In 11th Grade English and in AP Seminar, I have stopped treating polish as evidence that a student has done the reading. The evidence is the source work underneath the sentence.

When I say “evaluate the source,” I do not mean a scavenger hunt for a dot-org and a recent date. Students can pass that scavenger hunt and still misuse what they found. Evaluation is a decision about what the source is allowed to do inside an argument.

## A claim and a source are different objects

A student will write, “Social media harms teenagers,” and drop a link under it as if the link were a period. The link is not the claim. The link is a text with its own purpose, its own limits, and its own author. Before it can support anything, someone has to say what it is.

I ask three questions, out loud, until they become private:

1. Who is behind this, and what are they trying to accomplish?
2. What, exactly, is the sentence in front of me asserting?
3. What would a second, independent source have to confirm before I am willing to repeat it?

The third question is the one that changes the room. It tells students that confidence is adjustable. You can begin a search at sixty percent and finish at thirty, and that is a successful class period if you can explain the drop.

## What I have students do on a Monday

We take a claim they have already seen, often one that arrived as a caption or a headline. They write it in a single sentence, in their own words, before they open anything. If they cannot restate it, they are not ready to verify it. Then they open a second source that is not a cousin of the first: a primary document, a newsroom with a different method, a research summary that shows its data. They write what changed. Sometimes the claim survives and gets narrower. Sometimes it does not survive, and the notes from that failure become the most honest paragraph in the paper.

The full routine is the [four moves for a live claim](/resources/fact-checking-four-moves/). The companion for slower research is the [CLEAR source guide](/resources/source-credibility-guide/). I would rather students practice a short routine every week than complete one heroic checklist in October and never again.

## What a checklist cannot do

Checklists are useful. They are also easy to perform. A student can tick “author identified” without asking whether that author is in a position to know, or whether the piece is a study, a speech, or an ad. So the checklist in my room is a prompt for a sentence, not a column of boxes. “Author identified” is unfinished until the student writes who the author is and why that matters for this question.

I also stopped pretending that banning a tool is the whole policy. Students will meet fluent, uncredited text whether I permit the tool or not. The academic standard stays the same either way: if you cannot explain the source, you cannot use it. If you cannot explain the sentence, it is not yours yet.

## The argument is the trail

The papers I trust have a visible trail. A reader can see which source established the problem, which source complicated it, and where the student’s own commentary begins. That trail is the argument. The prose is how the trail is made readable.

If you teach writing, you already know the difference between a student who has a point and a student who has a mood. Source evaluation is how the point earns the right to be said in public. In an era of cheap fluency, that work is the assignment.
