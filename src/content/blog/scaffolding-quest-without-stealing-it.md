QUEST is easy to laminate and hard to teach. Question and Explore. Understand and Analyze. Evaluate Multiple Perspectives. Synthesize Ideas. Team, Transform, and Transmit. On a poster, it looks like five topics. In a student’s week, it has to be five moves, in an order, with a way to tell that one of them was skipped.

The failure I plan against is a beautiful organizer that becomes the essay. If the boxes are doing the thinking, the student will freeze when the boxes are gone. And the boxes do have to go. The Individual Written Argument is supposed to belong to the student.

## Name the move they are in

Early in the year I ask teams to say, in one sentence, which QUEST move they are practicing today. The sentence is surprisingly difficult. A group will say they are “researching” when they are actually still choosing a question, or they will start designing slides while their reports still disagree about the problem. Naming the move slows the performance of productivity and shows the gap.

The [QUEST planning map](/resources/quest-planning-map/) is deliberately plain. It asks for a question, what the student does not know, the perspectives in play, a working line of reasoning, and the audience. It does not ask for a thesis on day two. A thesis that arrives before the perspectives is usually a preference with footnotes.

## A question can be too large to be brave

A team will propose something like “How does climate change affect the world?” They are not being lazy. They are being loyal to a problem that matters, and they have not yet learned that scope is a form of respect for the reader. I ask who, specifically, has to make a decision, and what that person is deciding between. The question shrinks. The stakes usually get clearer, not smaller.

Then each student takes a lens and has to explain it in language a teammate can use. If the lens is “economic” and the notes are a pile of statistics with no claim, the lens has not been chosen yet. It has been named. We send them back. This is slower than assigning the lenses myself. It is also how a team learns that the division of labor is intellectual, not clerical.

## Stimulus materials are a conversation

When the stimulus packet arrives, students want to highlight everything and quote the calmest paragraph. I ask them to treat the packet as people who were not invited to agree. The [synthesis organizer](/resources/stimulus-synthesis-organizer/) has a column for how each source complicates the question. If that column is empty, the student is collecting, not synthesizing.

Synthesis is the move I coach most. Summary feels safe. Commentary feels exposed, because commentary is where the student can be wrong in public. I would rather they be precise and revisable than vague and finished.

## Take the scaffold down on purpose

Students are told which supports will disappear. The planning map is required on the first inquiry and optional on the second. By the individual argument, I still confer, and I still ask them to show a line of reasoning, but I do not hand them the structure. The [checklist](/resources/line-of-reasoning-checklist/) is a revision pass, used after a draft exists. It is not an outline generator.

College Board’s rubric remains the scoring authority. My scaffolds are the classroom translation: what the student can do on Tuesday so the official criteria are not a surprise in April. The aim is a researcher who can walk QUEST without looking at the poster, and who can tell you which move they are in when the work gets hard.
