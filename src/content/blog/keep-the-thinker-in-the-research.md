The risk I worry about is not a polished sentence. A polished sentence can be revised. The risk is a skipped judgment: the moment a student accepts a frame, a source list, or a counterargument they never had to build. Once that happens, the draft can look complete while the thinker has left the room.

I use emerging tools in class. I also keep a bright line around authorship. Tools can help students find, question, and revise. They do not get to own the claim. The [AI research protocol](/resources/ai-research-protocol/) is the version students keep in the front of the folder. This is the reasoning behind it.

## Think before the tool is open

Students write three things by hand, or in a document they cannot pretend the tool wrote:

- the question, in one sentence, with the scope visible
- the kind of evidence that would actually help
- two doubts they already have about their own hunch

If those boxes are empty, the tool is closed. This feels slow the first week. It is the whole point. A tool is most dangerous when a student does not yet know what they are looking for, because then any fluent answer will do.

I will allow a tool to suggest search terms, to translate a confusing paragraph into plainer language the student must then check, or to propose a counterargument the student has to go verify. I will not accept a tool’s paragraph as the analysis. The difference is whether the student’s judgment is still the thing being practiced.

## Verification is the assignment

Whatever a tool offers, a source has to exist outside it. Students retrieve the source, read the relevant part, and record whether the tool represented it fairly. Misrepresentation is a finding. It goes in the process notes. I would rather see a student catch a distorted summary than watch them cite a source they have not opened.

The process note is short on purpose:

- What did I ask the tool to do?
- What did I accept, reject, or rewrite?
- Which source confirmed the part I kept?

If a student cannot answer those, the material does not go in the paper. That rule applies to a search summary, a grammar suggestion that changes meaning, and a classmate’s outline. Convenience is not a citation.

## Voice is a test, not a vibe

I ask students to read two paragraphs aloud and mark any sentence they could not explain to the person across the table. The mark is not an accusation. It is a revision plan. Academic writing is allowed to sound formal. It is not allowed to sound like a person who is no longer present.

We talk, too, about privacy. Another student’s draft is not raw material for a public tool. Personal stories from a seminar stay in the room. The [classroom norms](/resources/generative-ai-classroom-norms/) put that in writing so I am not inventing the rule after something goes wrong.

## Why I do not outsource the ban

A total ban is simple to announce and hard to learn from. Students still need a way to tell a useful suggestion from a stolen thesis. They will need that way in college, at work, and in the group chat tonight. My course is a place to practice the distinction while a teacher can still ask, “Where did this reason come from?”

The standard I can say to a parent, an administrator, and a senior in the same words: the thinking in the paper has to be thinking the student can defend in the room. Tools that help them reach that standard can stay. Tools that replace it cannot.
